## The question before the council
Who is Atrest: Quit Vaping for first, what is it up against, and what one message should the launch carry? Stakes: one developer, launch is now, EU-only (EN/FR/NL), no analytics in the app, no health or outcome claims allowed, and the only revenue is the AI Guide (Raoul) after 20 free messages (12.99/month, 39.99/year).

## Seated: Dunford, Sutherland, Godin, Sharp, Hormozi (council session)
The owner's default bench. Dunford and Godin fit positioning; Sutherland fits the craving moment; Sharp is the natural dissenter on niche and differentiation; Hormozi is seated as the designated dissenter on the money side.

---

### April Dunford — positioning against real alternatives
Her first question: if Atrest didn't exist, what would these people do instead? Mostly: nothing but willpower, a puff counter or streak app, a general quit-smoking app, nicotine replacement from the pharmacy, or a free AI chatbot. In her framework, the status quo ("I'll just try harder") is the biggest competitor, and the pitch has to beat that, not a named rival.

Then: what can Atrest do that those can't? Not "AI" (chatbots are free) and not "tracking" (every counter does it). The unique attributes are concrete: a slip costs nothing, there are no scores, it needs no account, and it gives you something to do for the next three minutes. The value that follows: you can keep going after a bad day without the app making it worse. The best-fit customers are the ones who care a lot about that, which means people who've already tried and slipped, especially those who felt judged by a broken streak. She'd position inside the existing "quit vaping app" category (her "big fish, small pond" style), not invent a new category like "craving rituals", which she treats as the hardest and rarest path.

**Bottom line:** Be the quit-vaping app for people who've slipped before, where a slip costs nothing. Say it in the category people already search for.

### Rory Sutherland — psycho-logic of the craving moment
His question: what's the psychological problem, as opposed to the logical one? Logically, quitting is about days and counts. Psychologically, it's about getting through a few bad minutes without feeling like a failure. Changing how that moment *feels* is far cheaper than changing the habit itself, which is his "psychological moonshot" argument. A tree that grows while you wait reframes the craving as a pause that leaves something behind, rather than a test you pass or fail.

He'd point to the ghost of your old vape as the counterintuitive idea a committee would reject. It turns the vape into a sulky ex that fades away. That's funny, memorable and easy to talk about, so it's exactly the illogical element that, in *Alchemy*, separates a message people remember from a rational one. He'd also read the no-account, no-scores design as a costly signal of trust: you give up data and gamification on purpose, so people believe you mean it.

**Bottom line:** Sell the next three minutes, not the quit. Use the ghost as the thing people talk about.

### Seth Godin — smallest viable audience
His question: who's it for, and what's it for? Not "every vaper in the EU". It's for the smallest group that would miss Atrest if it vanished: people tired of apps that score them, who want something kind and quiet in the bad moment. In *This Is Marketing* he argues for delighting that group and letting them spread it. "People like us don't count streaks, we keep going" is a belief a small group can gather around.

Anonymity means no email list, so permission marketing in the usual sense is out. The product has to be the marketing (his *Purple Cow* argument). The tree you can share is the built-in way to remark on it. He'd warn hard against interruption ads and against any paywall pressure that betrays the generous free core.

**Bottom line:** Pick the people who've been hurt by streak apps, make something they'd show a friend, and let the shared tree do the spreading.

### Byron Sharp — dissenting on niche and differentiation
He'd say the niche talk is the classic mistake. Brands grow by reaching *all* category buyers, mostly light and new ones, and his duplication-of-purchase evidence says customer bases overlap heavily, so "our special tribe" is mostly a statistical illusion. Buyers rarely perceive fine differences like "no scores". What they notice is what comes to mind in the buying moment and what's easy to get.

For him, the job is availability:
- **Mental availability:** tie Atrest to the main entry point into the category, "the moment I want to vape". That's a buying situation every quitter has, slipped before or not.
- **Physical availability:** for an app, that means showing up in App Store search in French and Dutch. An English-only listing is invisible to much of Belgium, France and the Netherlands.
- **Distinctive assets:** choose one (the tree, Raoul or the ghost) and use it everywhere, consistently, so it's recognisable without the name.

**Bottom line:** Stop narrowing. Be findable in FR/NL, own "the moment you want to vape", and pick one distinctive asset.

### Alex Hormozi — designated dissenter on the money
He'd say the marketing debate is upstream of the real problem, which is the offer. Everything is free except the dog, so most people will never pay. And 12.99 a month for an AI chat competes with free chatbots. In his value equation you raise the dream outcome and certainty, and cut time and effort.

Here, EU and App Store rules block his usual levers: no outcome promises, no guarantees of results, no fake urgency. So only time and effort are left. Atrest's real edge is near-zero effort: three minutes, no typing. He'd push the annual price (39.99, a quarter of twelve monthly payments) as the main offer, and make clear what Raoul gives you that a generic chatbot doesn't. His other default diagnosis: you're not doing enough volume. One person can post short videos of trees growing and the ghost's messages every day.

Caveat: his playbook is built for high-ticket, pain-driven offers, and his scarcity tactics would break both EU rules and Atrest's voice.

**Bottom line:** Fix what makes people pay before polishing the message. Lead with annual, show why Raoul beats a free chatbot, and post daily.

## Where the council disagrees
1. **Narrow vs wide (Godin and Dunford vs Sharp).** Godin and Dunford want the people burned by streak apps. Sharp wants every EU vaper who wants to stop, reached in their own language. The real trade-off: a message that hits hard with a few people vs being findable by many. What would settle it: App Store Connect's own App Analytics (no SDK needed) on impressions and conversion per country after the FR/NL listings go live, plus a Product Page Optimization test of a "slipped before" screenshot set against a "the moment you want to vape" set.
2. **What to lead with (Sutherland and Sharp vs the owner's three features).** Sutherland and Sharp say one memorable thing (the ghost, or the tree) beats a list. The owner wants the tree, the walk and Raoul. The real trade-off: being remembered vs telling people everything that's in the app. What would settle it: a PPO test on the first three screenshots, which is where most decisions happen.
3. **Generosity vs monetisation (Godin vs Hormozi).** Godin says the free core *is* the marketing. Hormozi says it's why nobody will pay. The real trade-off: growth through goodwill vs revenue per user. What would settle it: of users who reach 20 messages with Raoul, how many subscribe in the first 4–6 weeks (App Store Connect sales and subscription data).
4. **Is "no scores" a differentiator? (Dunford vs Sharp).** Dunford builds the position on it. Sharp says buyers won't notice. What would settle it: real user words. If reviews and Reddit posts complain about streaks and shame, Dunford is right. That needs the customer-language research once the sites are reachable.

## Chair's synthesis
For a solo developer launching today with no ad tracking, the cheapest high-impact moves are availability first and a sharp message second.

- **Who first:** adults in BE/FR/NL who want to stop vaping and have tried before. Write for them, but don't exclude anyone (Sharp's point).
- **Up against:** willpower alone, counter and streak apps, and free AI chatbots (for Raoul). Nicotine replacement isn't a rival; the listing already points to a pharmacist.
- **One message:** *"For the moment you want to vape and don't want to."* It's the owner's own line. It owns the category entry point (Sharp), names the moment rather than a counter (Dunford), and stays clear of outcome claims. Supporting points: a slip costs nothing; no account.

- **Do:**
  1. Ship FR and NL listings with their own keywords. This is the biggest lever.
  2. Pick one distinctive asset and use it in the icon, the first screenshot and the share image. Recommended: the tree, because it's the first experience and it's shareable; let the ghost be the talkable detail.
  3. Make sure a shared tree image carries the app name, so word of mouth works without tracking.
  4. Run one PPO test on the first three screenshots once there's traffic.
- **Tripwires:** Hormozi: if very few users who hit 20 messages subscribe in the first 4–6 weeks, rework the Raoul offer (lead with annual, show what he does that a chatbot can't). Sharp: if FR/NL impressions stay low, message nuance doesn't matter yet.
- **Execute with:** `aso` (FR/NL keywords, screenshots), `product-marketing` (record the positioning once approved), then copy drafts by the lead.
- **Research to rerun when sites are reachable:** competitor listings (BE/FR/NL) and verbatim user language, to settle disagreements 1 and 4.
